1. Introduction

The Preparation phase is the foundation of the Incident Response (IR) lifecycle.

Before an organization experiences a cyberattack, it must ensure that its security team, processes, documentation, and technical infrastructure are ready to handle security incidents.

The main objective is to minimize the impact of an attack, reduce response time, preserve evidence, and ensure business continuity.

My understanding: Incident response does not start when an attack happens. It starts before the attack, through proper planning, monitoring, documentation, and security readiness.

2. What is Incident Response?

Incident Response is a structured approach used by organizations to identify, investigate, contain, eradicate, and recover from cybersecurity incidents.

It helps organizations:

Minimize security damage.

Reduce downtime and financial losses.

Protect sensitive information.

Preserve digital evidence.

Restore normal business operations.

Prevent similar incidents in the future.

Events vs Alerts vs Incidents

Term

Meaning

Example

Event

Any observable activity in a system or network.

User login

Alert

A notification generated by a security tool when suspicious activity is detected.

Multiple failed login attempts

Incident

A confirmed security violation requiring a response.

Confirmed unauthorized account access

Important SOC concept: Not every event is an alert, and not every alert is a confirmed security incident. An analyst must investigate and validate alerts before escalation.

3. Incident Response Frameworks

NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 2

The NIST Incident Response lifecycle consists of four major phases:

Preparation: Establish incident response capabilities before an incident occurs.

Detection and Analysis: Identify suspicious activity, investigate alerts, and determine the scope of an incident.

Containment, Eradication, and Recovery: Limit damage, remove threats, and restore affected systems.

Post-Incident Activity: Document lessons learned and improve security controls.

SANS PICERL

Another commonly referenced incident response model is SANS PICERL:

Preparation

Identification

Containment

Eradication

Recovery

Lessons Learned

Both frameworks provide a structured approach to incident handling.

4. People, Processes, and Technology

An effective Incident Response capability depends on three major pillars.

A. People

Organizations need trained security professionals who understand their responsibilities during an incident.

CSIRT – Cyber Security Incident Response Team

Typical responsibilities include:

SOC Analysts: Monitor alerts and investigate suspicious activity.

Incident Responders: Manage and coordinate incident handling.

Security Engineers: Maintain security infrastructure and detection capabilities.

IT Administrators: Support affected systems and infrastructure.

Legal and Compliance Teams: Handle regulatory and legal requirements.

Access should follow the Principle of Least Privilege (PoLP).

Security awareness training and phishing simulations also help employees recognize and report suspicious activity.

B. Processes

Organizations need documented procedures to ensure consistent incident handling.

Document

Purpose

Incident Response Plan

Defines the overall incident handling strategy and responsibilities.

Communication Plan

Defines who must be contacted and when.

Incident Response Playbooks

Provides step-by-step procedures for specific incidents.

Chain of Custody

Records the collection, handling, transfer, and storage of digital evidence.

Ticketing System

Maintains investigation records and actions taken.

Asset Inventory

Maintains information about organizational hardware and software.

Important concept – Chain of Custody:

Chain of Custody ensures that digital evidence is properly documented and its integrity and handling can be accounted for throughout an investigation.

C. Technology

Organizations need appropriate security tools to monitor, detect, investigate, and respond to threats.

Examples:

SIEM – Splunk, Microsoft Sentinel, Elastic Security.

EDR – Microsoft Defender for Endpoint, SentinelOne, CrowdStrike.

IDS/IPS – Suricata, Snort.

Vulnerability Management – Qualys, Nessus.

Network Monitoring – Wireshark, Zeek.

Digital Forensics – FTK Imager, Autopsy.

Jump Bag:

A Jump Bag is a collection of essential tools and resources that incident responders carry to support incident investigations.

It may contain forensic tools, storage devices, cables, adapters, and incident response documentation.

5. Visibility, Logging, and Detection

One of the most important lessons from this room is:

An organization cannot effectively investigate what it cannot see.

Visibility means having sufficient monitoring and logging across endpoints, servers, applications, and network infrastructure.

Role of SIEM in Visibility

A SIEM (Security Information and Event Management) solution collects and centralizes logs from different sources.

It helps SOC analysts:

Monitor security events.

Correlate activities across systems.

Identify suspicious patterns.

Generate security alerts.

Investigate potential incidents.

Build dashboards and reports.

Examples include Splunk, Microsoft Sentinel, and Elastic Security.

Real-World SOC Example

Consider a situation where an employee account experiences multiple failed login attempts followed by a successful login from an unusual location.

A SOC analyst should:

Check authentication logs.

Identify the source IP address.

Review the account's login history.

Correlate activity with other security logs.

Check for suspicious endpoint activity.

Determine whether the activity is legitimate or malicious.

Escalate the case if an incident is confirmed.

This demonstrates why proper logging and centralized monitoring are essential.

6. My Practical Understanding – SOC Preparation Checklist

Before an incident occurs, a security team should verify the following:

Incident Response Plan is documented.

CSIRT roles and responsibilities are assigned.

Incident escalation procedures are defined.

Asset inventory is maintained.

SIEM log collection is configured.

Critical endpoints have EDR coverage.

Windows and Linux logging is enabled.

Firewall and network logs are collected.

Detection rules are configured and tested.

Incident response playbooks are available.

Evidence collection procedures are documented.

Backup and recovery procedures are tested.
